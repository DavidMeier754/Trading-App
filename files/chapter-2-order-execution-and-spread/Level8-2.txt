2. Text: "A $0.05 Spread plus $0.02 of slippage on entry adds up fast when your target profit is only $0.20 per share.".
3. Multiple choice. Prompt: "Why is it useful to think of Spread and slippage together rather than separately?"Four answer options: (A) "They combine to form a big part of the real cost of a trade, which matters most for tight-margin Scalping" [correct], (B) "Only one of the two actually matters in practice", (C) "They cancel each other out automatically", (D) "They only apply to Limit Orders". Reveal note: "Together, these factors form a large part of the real-world cost of executing a trade."
4. Numeric scenario. Prompt: "A Scalper targets $0.25 profit. The Spread is $0.06, and slippage adds another $0.03 unfavorably on entry. Roughly what percentage of the target has been consumed before the trade has even moved in their favor?"Four answer options: (A) "About 36%" [correct], (B) "About 6%", (C) "About 3%", (D) "0% — Spread and slippage don't reduce targets". Reveal shows: "$0.06 + $0.03 = $0.09; $0.09 ÷ $0.25 = 36%."
5. True/False. Statement: "A trader who only checks the Spread before entering a trade has fully accounted for all their likely execution costs."Two answer options "True" / "False" (correct). Reveal note: "Checking Spread alone misses slippage effects — a common incomplete picture."
6. Scenario multiple choice. Prompt: "Given everything in this chapter so far, what general type of stock tends to minimize the COMBINED effect of Spread, slow execution, and slippage?"Four answer options: (A) "A highly liquid stock with tight Spreads and stable, fast-moving quotes" [correct], (B) "A low-liquidity stock with wide Spreads", (C) "Any stock at all — combined costs don't vary by stock", (D) "Only stocks trading above $1,000 per share". Reveal note: "Liquidity, tight Spreads, and stable execution all point toward the same favorable Scalping candidate."
